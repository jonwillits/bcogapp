**Part 2, Step 1 — Two Rules.** *It may help to reread the parts of the reading on the prisoner’s dilemma before this part: §11.1.7 (Games Without Minds), §11.1.8 (Repeated Encounters), and §11.2.1 (Playing a Repeated Game). **Part 2, Step 4 — A Rule That Predicts.** *Every entry so far is a fixed table of moves. This step adds a new strategy, called Predictor, that learns to predict its partner’s moves. For each partner, Predictor stores counts of what the partner did on round 1, and of what the partner did after each of the four outcomes of the previous round. Every count starts at one cooperation and one defection, so that no estimate is ever certain. From the counts, Predictor computes the probability that the partner cooperates next. On round 1 it cooperates. On every later round it works out, from those probabilities, the expected points of every sequence of its own moves over the next 3 rounds (8 sequences), and plays the first move of the best sequence. With probability 5% it plays the other move instead. This is an “exploration” move, a probe, designed to get information in the event that the moves are always the same. Predictor starts its learning over with every new partner. It is never given a partner’s rule, only the counts.* / *Predictor does, as a program, what you did in Part 1: it predicts each round and chooses each round. Because its probes fall at random, its matches come out differently on every run, so its results are means over 50 runs, with the spread. The step can be run again at any time, and each run plays new matches.* / *First, try to guess where Predictor will rank in the round-robin of Step 3. Which partner will it do best against, and which worst? Then press “Run with Predictor”. The panel shows the ranking and, for each partner, Predictor’s points. It shows its share of the best possible total, and the points it lost to probing. The panel also shows a replay of Predictor against Defect forever after one defection, with the first probe marked; Predictor’s estimates for any partner, as four rows beside the partner’s true table; and your own Part 1 results against the same six partners, over matches of the same lengths.* About 5 minutes; leave any time. Content: *Predictor estimates each partner’s next move from the partner’s past moves. Tit-for-tat estimates nothing.* *Finding out costs points: a probe is a defection, and some partners answer it for the rest of the match.* Predictions: *Where will Predictor rank among the eight entries?* [first / top three / in the middle / bottom three / last]; *Against which partner will Predictor do best?* [Always cooperate / Always defect / Tit-for-tat / Defect forever after one defection / Win-stay, lose-shift / Suspicious tit-for-tat]; *Against which partner will Predictor do worst?* [Always cooperate / Always defect / Tit-for-tat / Defect forever after one defection / Win-stay, lose-shift / Suspicious tit-for-tat]

**Part 2, Step 5 — Mistakes.** *In this step, a noise setting is used to insert randomness into the behavior of the strategies. Above 10%, the strategies become very hard to tell apart.* / *The round-robin of Step 4 runs again at the noise set on the left, and the table shows each number with its change from no noise. **Part 2, Step 6 — Generations.** *In this step, we will do another competition. This time, there are many competitors. The different strategies each start as an equal share of the population. Each generation, the ratio of the different strategies in the population changes to reflect the relative success of the different strategies. For example, if the tit-for-tat players together earn 30% of all the points earned in a generation, tit-for-tat will be 30% of the population in the next generation. This resembles Axelrod’s original ecological simulation of different strategies. The results plot will show every entry’s share over 300 generations. It will show the results with no noise, and with the noise set in Step 5, side by side.* / *The simulation will also show how the performance of the different strategies changes if you factor in that more complex strategies would require bigger brains, and therefore more energy. A strategy that always cooperates or always defects would need no memory at all. Tit-for-tat and the other rules would need a limited memory, what happened on the previous round, which is one number. Predictor would need a larger memory to keep track of the history: its ten counts and the previous round, which is eleven numbers. The memory is charged for: a cost per number remembered, per round, set on the stage at 0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, or 0.1 points, is subtracted from each strategy’s average points before each generation’s update. The results plot will show how the spread of the different strategies changes as this cost rises. The results panel marks the lowest cost at which Predictor’s share, and at which Tit-for-tat’s share, falls below its starting share by generation 300.* / *First, predict which entry vanishes first, which is most common at the end with mistakes, and at what cost Predictor stops spreading. Then press “Run the generations”.* About 3 minutes; leave any time. Content: *This cost stands in for what memory costs an animal, as a larger brain costs energy. It is not a measured cost of any brain.* Predictions: *With no mistakes, which entry vanishes first?* [Always cooperate / Always defect / Tit-for-tat / Defect forever after one defection / Win-stay, lose-shift / Suspicious tit-for-tat / My rule / Predictor]; *With mistakes, which entry is most common at the end?* [Always cooperate / Always defect / Tit-for-tat / Defect forever after one defection / Win-stay, lose-shift / Suspicious tit-for-tat / My rule / Predictor]; *At what cost per number remembered, if any, does Predictor stop spreading with mistakes?* [0 / 0.01 / 0.02 / 0.05 / 0.1 / it does not spread even at no cost / it spreads at every cost]

**Part 2, Step 7 — The cycle.** *In Step 7, you will see a simulated world of different players following different strategies, which takes proximity and spatial location into account. There is a grid of 40 × 40 cells, wrapping at the edges, so that the far left cells touch the far right cells, and the top cells touch the bottom cells; a setting on the left makes the edges stop instead, so that a cell on an edge has fewer neighbors. Each cell contains a player, playing one of the rules checked on the left. Which rule is in which cell is color-coded and shown in the key.* / *In this simulation, all three steps of natural selection are simulated. First, competition. Instead of playing against all the other strategies, each player only plays against its eight neighbors. They play each neighbor for 10 rounds, at the noise set in Step 5. Its score is its average points over its neighbors.* / *Next, we simulate selection. The highest performing players survive and reproduce, replacing their lower performing neighbors. The exact rule: each cell takes the rule of the highest-scoring cell among itself and its eight neighbors, so a cell keeps its rule when no neighbor scored more, and a tie is broken at random.* / *Finally, additional variation is inserted by allowing for a small amount of mutation. Each cell has a chance of switching to a rule drawn at random from the checked rules, with the probability set on the left.* / *To start, run the simulation using the three preselected rules: Always cooperate, Always defect, and Tit-for-tat. **The six rules, as the diagnosis lists them:**

- Always cooperate: cooperates on every round
- Always defect: defects on every round
- Tit-for-tat: cooperates on round 1, then plays your move from the previous round
- Defect forever after one defection: cooperates until the first round on which you defect, then defects on every later round
- Win-stay, lose-shift: cooperates on round 1; after a round that gave it 3 or 5 points, repeats its own last move; after a round that gave it 0 or 1 points, switches
- Suspicious tit-for-tat: defects on round 1, then plays your move from the previous round

**The five animals, as the diagnosis lists them:**

- Barriers: goes to the piece nearer a barrier, opaque or clear (separated by T2)
- Gaze: goes to the piece farther from where the dominant is facing; with the dominant’s door shut, has no preference (separated by T3)
- Line of sight now: goes to the piece outside the dominant’s line of sight from its door, open or shut (separated by T4, T5 and T6)
- Line of sight before: goes to the piece the present dominant did not watch being placed or moved, and otherwise to the piece outside its line of sight (separated by T4, T5 and T6)
- Learner: predicts from a history of contests which piece the dominant is less likely to take, from layout and behavior alone, and goes there (separated by T8)

## 3. The models, as they shipped

**Payoffs** (`exact.ts`): exact expected payoffs for two memory-one rules over R rounds at noise ε by a four-state chain over the previous round's two moves, with each intended move flipped with probability ε; held to the mean of 10,000 simulated matches within 1% for every pair at 0% and 5%. **The best total** (`best.ts`): dynamic programming over the rule's state (the previous round from its side, or nothing on round 1), checked against brute force for every rule to 12 rounds. **Predictor**: per partner, counts of the first move and of the move after each of the four outcomes (from its own side), each from (1, 1); the estimate the share of cooperations; round 1 cooperate; after that the expected points of each of the 8 sequences of its own moves over the next 3 rounds under the estimates, by enumerating the partner's branches, the first move of the best, ties to cooperate; a probe with probability 5%; its prediction before each round the move with the larger estimate (cooperate at 0.5). **The round-robin**: every entry against every entry including itself for 200 rounds; rule pairs exact; pairs with Predictor played once per seed over 50 seeds, the mean with its sd; an entry's total the mean over opponents including itself. **The generations**: share × average points against the population, renormalized, 300 generations; Predictor's cost subtracted from its average, with the product floored at 0. **The grid**: scores from the exact 3 × 3 table at the current noise and 10 rounds; a takeover counted when a rule passes half the grid after another held it. **Inverse planning** (`paths/models.ts`): M2 with β 2.0 and γ 0.25, the planner's softmax over the nine actions' values, a goal change a draw from the prior (so H, with γ 1 and β 2.5, depends on the last step alone). **The learner**: Φ = a − p on the Prediction setting, one layer, `learnWeights` only. **The cells**: Φ = a − p with a the motor drive and p the visual prediction, every step of the loop while learning is on, `learnWeights` only.

## 8. Where the build departed from the spec, or where its targets turned out wrong

1. **The baiting in the standard tests** (§8.2, §8.3): the spec reads as if the dominant watches every placement through its door. With that, in T1 the dominant would go to the last piece it watched placed, the hidden one as often as not, and in T2 it would always take the piece placed last. The standard tests (T1, T2, T3, T7, T8) are baited with the dominant's door down, and the dominant sees the arena through its door only afterward, as in Hare et al. (2000); T4 to T6 are baited with it watching, as in Hare et al. (2001). The dominant's "watched" is its door open or at viewing during the placement or move, wherever the piece was put.
2. **T6** (§8.3): "as T5 without the move; the dominant that watched is replaced by one that did not" leaves both pieces unwatched by the present dominant, and no rule could prefer one. Here the first piece is placed with the first dominant watching, the dominant is replaced, and the second piece is placed with the new one watching, so exactly one piece is unwatched by the present dominant, which is what Hare et al. (2001, Experiment 2) varied.
3. **The learner's no-preference band**: two predictions within 0.1 of each other count as no preference and the learner goes at random, like any animal whose rule gives none. Without it the learner's seed-to-seed spread on T2 and T8 was a coin flip decided by hairline differences.
4. **No hidden layer**: one layer learned T1–T7 in the usual room and generalized to T8 from many rooms; the hidden layer added nothing (§4.7), so §13 item 8's exception is not used.
5. **Part 2, Step 1's four predictions** are asked together and committed before the four runs, rather than one before each run.
6. **The grid's takeover count** is of takeovers (a rule passing half the grid after another held it), not of every flicker of the most common rule; the flicker count ran to about 170 per 1,000 generations and the takeover count matches the spec's 3 to 8.
7. **The cells' own-hand view** is present on 3 in 10 of own grasps, not every one: with every own grasp seen, the Prediction rule credits the own view fully and the seen copy learns nothing (Lab 5's blocking). The copy begins 0.2 s after the student's grasp, "a moment later", and the three constants were swept once before the targets were set (§4.8).
8. **Predictor's "points lost to probing"** is negative against both tit-for-tats: without probes it falls into mutual defection with them from its first wrong guesses and stays there; a probe breaks the lock. The panel's note says so.
9. **Predictor spreads over generations only with noise**: without noise it vanishes at every cost, including none, because the always-cooperators it exploits vanish first and against the rest it does worse than tit-for-tat. The cost setting is still shown and the finding reported, as §6.6 asks.
14. **The cost is charged to every entry by its memory, not to Predictor alone** (Jon, 2026-11-02): §6.6's cost on Predictor misstated the ladder, since the memory-one rules store the previous round and the two fixed rules store nothing. The units are 0, 1 and 11, the cost per stored number 0 to 0.1 a round, and the panel reports where Predictor and where Tit-for-tat stop spreading (§4.4).
15. **The grid holds any set of the entries, and its edges can stop** (Jon, 2026-11-02): §6.7's three rules are the default and the measured setting; the student can check any of the eight, Predictor included (its encounters played, 50 seeds, for the table), and switch the edges from wrapping to stopping, both measured in §4.5. A notes box on the stage asks what happens locally and with each combination.
16. **Steps 4 and 5 can be run again**, each run on new seeds, so that Predictor's numbers move within their spread (Jon, 2026-11-02); Step 3 shows any entry's record round by round.
10. **The paths' models**: a goal change in M2 is a draw from the prior, which may return the same goal (the Appendix is not in the library); this is the reading on which H with γ = 1 depends on the last step alone, as the paper's text says.
11. **Part 3's Step 1 reveal comes with Step 2's**, at the end of the part, since the films' commit is already final and the two reveals read together; Step 1's results are saved at its commit.
12. **The simulated classes** are all pending (§6); the panels are built and read from the data file.
